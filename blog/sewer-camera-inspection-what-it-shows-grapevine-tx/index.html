A camera on a flexible cable shows what is wrong with a sewer line without digging. Knowing what to look for makes the video worth watching.

What a sewer camera inspection finds, and why it is worth it

A sewer camera inspection sends a waterproof camera through your drain line and records what it sees. The plumber watches the screen live, marks where problems are and gives you a recording. It turns a guess into a diagnosis.

People book one when drains back up repeatedly, before buying a house or after a clog that keeps coming back. The camera shows whether the cause is roots, grease, a collapsed section or a simple blockage.

Here is what the camera can show, how to read it and what to ask the plumber afterward.

What a camera can find

Each problem looks different on screen.

Tree roots

Fine roots enter at joints and grow into thick masses. They look like hair or webbing.

Grease and buildup

Hardened grease narrows the pipe. It looks like a rough coating.

Belly or sag

A low spot holds water and waste. The camera shows standing water.

Cracks and breaks

A crack looks like a dark line. A break can show soil or a gap.

Offset joints

Pipe sections that have shifted do not line up.

Collapse

The pipe is crushed or blocked by soil and cannot be passed.

How the inspection runs

The visit is usually short.

1

Find an access point

A cleanout, a toilet removal or a roof vent gives a way in.

2

Feed the camera

The plumber pushes the camera along the pipe and watches the screen.

3

Mark and measure

A locator on the camera finds where each problem sits and how deep.

4

Review and record

You see the video, and the plumber explains the options.

What the findings mean for the next step

The camera tells you which fix fits.

When to book an inspection

Book one when drains back up more than once, when more than one fixture is slow, when you smell sewage near the yard or when you are buying an older house. A camera before you buy tells you what the seller's inspection may not, and the video can support a price negotiation.

Book one after a clog is cleared too, if it was a main line backup. The camera confirms that the cause is gone.

What to ask afterward

Ask for the recording and for the location of every problem. Ask how far along the line each is and how deep. Ask whether the plumber can repair what you saw, and what the price is before work starts. A plumber who explains what they saw and what each choice costs is one you can trust. Also see sewer inspection for what a visit includes.

Limits of a camera inspection

A camera shows the inside of the pipe, but it cannot see through a fully blocked line, and a very tight bend or a transition can stop it. A plumber may clear the pipe first and then inspect, so the camera can pass.

The camera also cannot test whether a joint leaks under pressure. If the picture shows a joint that looks tight but you still see signs of leaking, further testing may be needed. Ask what the camera could not see, and what that means for the next step.

How to get the most from the video

A few habits make the recording more useful.

1

Ask for footage and a locate

Get the video and a note of where each problem sits and how deep.

2

Note the pipe material

The camera usually shows whether the pipe is clay, cast iron, plastic or another type.

3

Save it for later

Keep the file with your house records, because the next buyer or plumber may want it.

4

Compare quotes

Share the same video with two plumbers so you are comparing the same facts.

Reading the signs before you book

Some warning signs show up well before a line fails. Gurgling in a tub when a toilet is flushed, drains that clear and then slow again, damp or sunken ground over the line and a sewage smell in the yard all deserve a look. Several slow fixtures at once usually mean the main line, not a single drain.

If you own an older house, a camera pass is a sensible habit every few years. It costs little compared with a collapsed line, and the video gives you a baseline to compare against later.

Questions homeowners ask

Can a camera find a leak in a sewer line?

It can show cracks, breaks and standing water. A leak that is not visible may need other tests.

Will I get a recording of the inspection?

Ask for it. A good plumber provides one and explains what it shows.

Is a camera inspection worth it when buying a house in Grapevine?

It is a smart check on an older line. It shows roots, sags and breaks that a visual inspection cannot.

Does Grapevine bill sewer by water use?

The city excluded February 2021 from its winter sewer rates after the storm. Call the city at (817) 410-3000 and ask how your sewer charge is calculated.

How long does an inspection take?

Often under an hour for a typical house, longer if the line is long or blocked.

A camera inspection replaces guessing with evidence. Watch the video, ask where each problem is and choose a fix with the facts in hand.
